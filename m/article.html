Control That Means Something

I looked at 26 clinical AI companies. They all write about governance. Almost none of them show the screen where a person actually uses it.

Diana Gordon Product designer, human oversight in clinical AI September 2026 · 7 min read
Part 1The good news Part 2The hard part Part 3What to build

This summer I went looking for one thing.

Not AI policy. Not risk frameworks. I wanted the screen. The moment a clinician sees that an AI is unsure. The moment an agent stops and asks instead of guessing. The moment a person is supposed to take control.

So I read everything public I could find from the companies putting AI into clinical and research work: product pages, docs, safety frameworks, conference announcements, even the job posts they use to hire designers.

Here’s what I found. The governance is written down everywhere. The interface for it is written down almost nowhere.

That gap is what this piece is about. I think it’s one of the most interesting open problems in clinical software right now.

The lens I used

Boundary. What the agent can touch, what it can’t, and where an irreversible action has to stop.

Control. Real authority at the right level, not a stream of approvals that turns into rubber-stamping.

Legibility. Enough evidence on screen that a person knows when to trust, question or step in.

Where this comes from

These three words build on older human factors work: levels of automation (Sheridan and Verplank), Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens on types and levels of automation (2000), Bainbridge on the ironies of automation, and Microsoft’s Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction. What I add is a rule for agents in clinical work: irreversibility sets the boundary, and confirmation has to be earned by looking.

Part 1

The good news: the pieces already exist

A few companies have shipped oversight patterns that are genuinely good. They’re worth naming, because the rest of the field will borrow them.

Check the source when you need it

Abridge writes clinical notes from the visit conversation. Highlight any sentence in a note and the matching part of the transcript lights up. Press play and you hear the audio it came from. The trail ends at what the patient actually said. Confirmed

When you’re not checking, it disappears. No confidence badge, no superscript. It costs zero pixels until you ask.

That’s a real design choice. A badge that stays on screen helps you scan and audit. Checking on demand keeps the note clean, but only helps if you already suspect something. Which one fits which clinical task? Nobody has published an answer.

Ask before guessing

Flatiron Health’s Telescope does something rarer. A researcher types “TNBC patients” (a breast cancer subtype), and the system asks back: how do you want to define HER2 status, and with which tests? Confirmed

Every AI system makes calls like this all the time, silently. Telescope turns the call into a question the person answers before anything runs. It catches the error review is worst at: a query that ran perfectly on the wrong definition.

It also shows its work twice, in plain language and as raw SQL, side by side, so a clinician and a data scientist can check the same result. And Flatiron published that accuracy started at 50 to 60 percent and passed 90 percent after iteration. Sharing the starting number says a lot about a team.

Autonomy as a setting

Medable’s Agent Studio lets teams choose, agent by agent, whether it runs on its own or under human review. All of this sits inside a regulated environment (GxP and 21 CFR Part 11, the FDA rules for electronic records). Confirmed

That raises a hard question almost nobody is talking about. If half your agents are supervised and half aren’t, how does a study lead tell, at a glance, which numbers on the screen passed through a human? Medable ships the setting. As far as I can find, it hasn’t shown what it looks like.

Three companies solved three different parts of oversight. None of them solved the part you can see.

What everyone is converging on

Look across the field and the same moves keep showing up.

Three ways to correct the machine. Abridge has you check against the source. Nabla lets you fix a note by asking in plain language. Suki keeps corrections in voice. One is built for auditing, one for speed, one for staying hands-free. Nobody has said which fits which task. Confirmed

A second model does the watching. Hippocratic AI pairs its main model with more than thirty supervising models. Google’s AI Co-Clinician uses a planner that keeps a second agent within safe limits. Anthropic replaces per-action approval with a classifier and hard stops on irreversible actions. So if a model is watching, what does the human see? Confirmed

Live monitoring over scheduled audits. Systems drift every day, and audits happen on a calendar. ECRI, the patient-safety nonprofit, opened its national reporting network to suspected AI errors. It had found that nearly a third of health leaders saw wrong AI output in the past year, and 9 percent saw an AI error reach a patient. Confirmed

The boundary is the feature. OpenAI’s health assistant reads from Epic and never writes back. Cedar keeps its agent to billing and sends anything clinical to a person. Nabla keeps the audio on the device. Each limit becomes something you can check, as long as you can see it holding. Confirmed

Risk tiers, not on or off. Included Health sorts guidance into standard, high-risk and emergency, each with a different level of clinician review. That’s a design system in all but name, yet nothing published shows how a patient experiences the tiers. Confirmed

Patients want a say. In a Pew survey of 3,488 US adults, 63 percent want more say over AI in their care, 46 percent aren’t sure whether it was used at all, and 72 percent say disclosure is very or extremely important. Disclosure just became a product surface. Confirmed

Part 2

The hard part: more review isn’t the answer

It would be easy to say clinical AI just needs more human review. The best evidence says the opposite.

In August 2026, Anthropic published results from a controlled study with 1,053 paid professional testers. People approved 97 percent of permission prompts. Human review caught 13.6 percent of dangerous commands. Automated review caught 89 percent. The study tested coding agents, not clinical staff, but the pattern is the one clinical teams describe.

And it got worse over time. Early in a session, people blocked about 17 percent of dangerous commands. After fifty or more prompts, about 5 percent. Automated review held steady.

That’s alert fatigue, finally measured. Clinical designers have been saying it for years.

The number that changes the job

In the same data, people rejected only 3 percent of single steps, but 39 percent of whole plans.

People are bad at approving actions and good at judging a plan.

So oversight doesn’t fail. Oversight at the wrong level fails. The fix isn’t an approval on every step. It’s fewer, bigger decisions: approve the plan before the agent acts, limit what an unreviewed action can break, and escalate only when it really needs a person. That’s why Anthropic’s Plan Mode is the pattern to watch. You review the whole plan up front instead of clicking yes fifty times.

The gap, plainly

Every company I looked at publishes governance policy. Across all 26, I found no public documentation of how they show confidence, how they surface a failure, how an agent says it doesn’t know, what the screen shows while an agent is still thinking, or how you recover from an error.

The governance is written down everywhere. The interface that would let a clinician trust it is written down nowhere.

There are partial exceptions. Abridge has a public stance on preventing made-up content, but it’s a method, not a screen. Flatiron’s clarifying question is described in a trade article, not by Flatiron. Verily is the only company where I could confirm both data provenance and audit trails as named features.

Accessibility barely comes up. I found no accessibility conformance claim from any major scribe or clinical-assistant company. Verily was the one exception, and only because it’s in their design job posts. In clinics, with gloves, bright rooms, shared workstations and long shifts, that silence is a finding of its own.

To be fair, a lot of this work sits behind clinician logins, so I couldn’t see it. But that’s the point. Governance gets published because buyers and regulators ask for it. The interface serves the clinician deciding whether to accept one sentence, and nobody has designed for that person in public yet.

Part 3

What to build next

If the gap is real, the work is specific. And it’s design work, not policy work.

Boundary · Control · Legibility

Boundary. Show what an agent can touch, what’s read-only, and which irreversible actions can’t happen without a person. When some agents are supervised and some aren’t, show which outputs went through a human.

Control. Put human authority where it counts: when a request is ambiguous, before a big sequence runs, and when the system reaches the edge of its scope. Dozens of tiny prompts aren’t control.

Legibility. Make provenance, uncertainty, escalation and patient disclosure readable. Not more chrome. Just enough evidence, at the right moment, to know when to look closer and how to step in.

Working demonstration

I built one version of this: a screening agent that logs reversible facts on its own and holds an irreversible decision for a person. Watch the boundary work →

Here’s the shift in one line. For most of software’s history, oversight lived in a document and a backend. With AI agents in clinical work, oversight is something a person has to see, question and act on in the moment.

That makes it an interface. And that interface is still mostly unbuilt.

I think that’s a great place to be working.

How I did this

Public product pages, docs, safety and governance frameworks, peer-reviewed and trade reporting, and job posts, gathered June to September 2026. Many products sit behind clinician logins or demos, so I worked from published material rather than direct use. I didn’t describe any screen I hadn’t read about in the company’s own sources. Every claim is tagged by how well I could verify it.
