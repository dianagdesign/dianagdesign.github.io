The goal. Cut the time it takes to activate a clinical study. Coordinators were typing the same study into many systems by hand.

My role. Research and design, from the first interviews to the prototype now in development.

The hard part. Letting agents take over the paperwork without taking decisions away from the people accountable for the study.

Where it stands. Leadership approved the direction, then spent over an hour on the prototype in a later review. It is now in development. Requirements are written from my prototype, and the current build is deliberately narrow. Task-based testing with coordinators is the next step.

The real cost was typing the same study again and again

Every study ran through many disconnected systems, each with its own login and manual entry. Nearly three in four specialists surveyed named fragmented systems as a top cause of delay, and more than four in five said one shared tracking view would change their work. Every one of them spent time each week chasing status that did not move a study forward. A status tracker I designed earlier made the delays visible and showed where they started: at intake. That is where the fix had to begin.

“I would really love to not have to enter the same data into multiple places. That is the single biggest thing.”Research protocol specialist

Every role asked for the same thing: one place to see where a study stands

Over six months I ran standing research panels with the three roles that carry a study to launch. Three findings set the direction.

I kept going back to protocol specialists, research coordinators and activation project managers with surveys, interviews, walkthrough sessions and prototype reviews.

Protocol specialists

Status lived in too many places. Their top request in prototype reviews was one view of every study and who is covering it.

Research coordinators

Unclear task ownership and buried notifications were the main blockers. Two in three rated a personal work queue “extremely valuable.”

Project managers

They didn’t want to manage studies in another tool. They wanted to stop chasing status by hand across parallel workstreams.

One front door for study teams, with agents doing the work underneath

I proposed the structure for the next phase of the product and scoped it to the stretch where three teams work in parallel and status gets lost: from the moment documents are ready to the day a study opens for enrollment.

One coordinator first, on purpose

In prototype review, a coordinator who runs investigator-initiated trials, where the institution is the sponsor, could not find her work in the flow I had built. The other reviewers worked on industry-sponsored studies, and the prototype fit them.

The platform supports many study types and roles, and building for one persona first was a debated choice. I made the case for focusing on the coordinators who most directly drive activation time, the ones running industry-sponsored studies, and the current build centers on them. Investigator-initiated trials still appear in the study list without their own designed flow.

It was a hard call to keep making. The build is now focused on that one coordinator. The cost is real: other study types and roles are not in the current build.

One prototype, two scopes

Once development started, reviews kept turning into debates about what belonged in the current build. I added a scope switch to the prototype, with the current build on one side and the long-term design on the other, so the team could see each decision in place.

Open questions went to the PM and the business analyst as two or three working options, each with its tradeoffs and my recommendation. Most were settled in the same session. Where an answer depended on what the data could support, I designed a fallback beside the ideal design, so the build did not have to wait.

Once the software did the typing, the question became: who decides?

I walked coordinators through early concepts of each step, then took the direction to leadership, who approved it. The model is simple: nothing moves from proposal to action without a person.

Three rules keep a person in charge

Boundary, control and legibility are the framework from my essay, Control That Means Something, applied to this product.

Boundary

Agents propose, people approve

The agent suggests which service areas a study needs. No agent prepares answers until a coordinator approves the plan.

Control

Stopping is within reach

Pause, stop and rerun sit in each agent’s row menu and on its review page, so stepping in never means hunting through settings.

Legibility

Say whose move it is

Every answer shows the protocol line behind it. Every step names what it is waiting on and whose move it is, the coordinator’s or the agent’s.

Leadership approved it, and the team is building it

It’s now in development. Requirements are written from my prototype, and I maintain a design repo and component guide for the developers, built on the enterprise design system. When the developers sent questions from the code, I answered them in the next release of the repo and checked their branch against the spec.

How I’ll know it works. How coordinators approve, correct and stop the agents is written as one set of rules, so whoever builds the agents builds against the same ones. Bulk approval by confidence level is deliberately parked until workshops and testing show people review first. The design keeps edits and feedback apart, so the AI team can tell a wrong answer from a local decision. No agent has run in production yet, so the first measures are still ahead. Once the agents run, I plan to measure override rate and time per confirmation to catch rubber-stamping.

The thinking behind this work: Control That Means Something, on designing human oversight for AI that acts on its own.
